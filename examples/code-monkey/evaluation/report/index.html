Code Monkey, transcribed by an AI agent and checked against the master tracks

Claude Opus 5.5, driving the ai-arranger skills in Claude Code, turned the mixed recording of Jonathan Coulton's "Code Monkey" into a full band score with a lead sheet and drum, bass and guitar sheets, in under an hour. We then measured the result against the song's official unmixed tracks and against fan transcriptions found online. The sheets below are shown exactly as the agent produced them; nothing was corrected afterwards. The errors are the point of this page.

What was produced

Five sheets, each as PDF and MusicXML (in sheets/; click a page to open its PDF). The band sheets use repeat barlines, 1st and 2nd endings, one-bar repeat signs and multi-measure rests; a playback check confirms each short sheet plays exactly the same notes as the written-out score. Measure numbers in the written-out score are the recording's bar numbers, so every bar can be checked against the audio.

Also produced: the written-out versions, MIDI, 13 audio mock-ups (General MIDI), automatic checks (qa.txt), text groove cards and a chords-and-words sheet. Two tools were added to the skills on the way: compact.py (short scores with repeats, checked against the written-out playback) and barmap.py --grid (exact bar grid for records made on a click).

How it was made

One request in plain words: "produce a lead sheet of Code Monkey, a drum sheet, guitar sheet if required and bass sheet, then a MusicXML nicely written with all the instruments, sing and backing vocals". The agent cannot hear; it reads the recording through measurements: separated stems, pitch tracks, spectra, onset grids and piano-roll images.

Why it went wrong: four families of error

Every error found above fits one of four causes. The first is a limit of the tools: they hear a mixed recording. The second and third are the agent's method, and those are fixable. As of this report they are rules in the skill. The fourth is about checking itself.

1 · The tools hear a mix

Four stems hold everything: five overdriven guitars land in one stem, so the agent counted guitars from mixed spectra and read the broadband attacks of distorted 8th notes as an acoustic guitar strumming. A palm-muted E rings through every verse bar and hides chord stabs shorter than half a bar. Power chords have no third, so A against A minor rests on a faint note, and distortion adds false partials; it can even fake a bass, because an overdriven fifth makes a difference tone an octave below its root. Partly fixable: a 6-stem model with a guitar stem, stereo position (guitars 4 and 5 are a left/right pair), and asking the person to confirm the line-up.

2 · The method stopped too early or decided too coarsely

Chords were labelled per half bar, from the loudest pitches, while the bass line in the same score already had the right riff. The search for backing voices stopped at the first one found (the octave double), so a harmony a fifth above went unwritten. Lyric conflicts were settled by trusting one source instead of counting the sung notes. And nothing was cross-checked before delivery, although four fan charts would have flagged the verse harmony, both A minors and both lyric errors.

3 · Notation choices were made silently

Blue notes were bent into the key, slides written as their target, notes held through breaths, ghost notes and fills left out. Each is a defensible choice for a sheet a musician reads, and together they cost about 10 points of pitch agreement and 20 to 35 points of drum recall. The error is that the sheets don't say so.

4 · Checking needs the same care

The first drum check used plain frequency bands on the master track and counted the snare's low end as kicks, reporting 58% kick recall instead of 79%. Even with master tracks, the yardstick has to be measured. Beyond that, the agent cannot listen: everything was decided from numbers and pictures, and the General MIDI mock-up can't show tone or feel.
